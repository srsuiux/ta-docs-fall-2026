Introductory Computer Science (CS1) students often know basic programming syntax, including variables, data types, input/output, conditional statements, loops, and basic program structure. However, knowing programming syntax does not necessarily mean that students can independently solve programming problems.

When presented with a well-defined programming problem, students must make several decisions before and during implementation. They need to understand the requirements, identify necessary information, develop an algorithmic approach, determine the required logic, organize the execution flow, and verify their solutions.

AI programming tutors can assist students throughout this process. However, providing too much assistance may cause students to rely on AI-generated solutions rather than develop their own problem-solving abilities.

This creates an important research question:

Which programming decisions should AI support or expose, and which decisions should remain the student's responsibility to preserve active reasoning and independent problem-solving ability?

The objective is not to prevent students from receiving AI assistance. Instead, it is to investigate how AI can support students without taking ownership of the problem-solving process away from them.

2. Programming Decisions in CS1
When solving a well-defined programming problem, students make several interconnected decisions.

The following is a proposed classification of these decisions for the study.

Programming decision What the student needs to determine
Problem understanding What is the problem asking me to accomplish?
Input identification What information does the program need?
Output identification What should the program produce?
Data modeling How should the information be represented?
Problem decomposition What smaller tasks make up the problem?
Algorithmic approach What steps are necessary to solve the problem?
Computational logic What calculations and operations are required?
Conditional reasoning What situations or conditions must be considered?
Control-flow selection Should the solution use sequence, selection, or iteration?
Execution order What needs to happen first, next, and last?
Testing strategy What scenarios and boundary cases should be tested?
These categories are a proposed operational framework, not an established standardized taxonomy.

The distinction between syntax and problem-solving
A student might know how to write an if-else statement but still struggle to determine:

Whether a condition is necessary.
Which condition should be evaluated.
Which comparison operator is appropriate.
What should happen when the condition is true or false.
Whether multiple conditions are necessary.
How the different operations should be organized.
The challenge is not necessarily knowing how to write the syntax. It is deciding when and why to use it.

3. Levels of Problem Representation
An important distinction is between understanding what a problem requires and knowing exactly how to implement its solution.

Four levels can help explain this distinction.

Level 1: Problem statement
Describes the requirements, constraints, and expected outcomes.

Example:

Develop a payroll program that calculates an employee's weekly gross pay, accounting for regular working hours and overtime.

Level 2: High-level problem representation
Describes the major responsibilities of the solution without exposing detailed implementation logic.

Example:

Collect employee working information.
Determine the appropriate pay calculation.
Calculate the employee's earnings.
Produce the final payroll result.
This communicates what the program needs to accomplish without revealing the specific conditions, formulas, or execution steps.

Level 3: Detailed algorithm or pseudocode
Describes how the solution should work.

Example:

INPUT hourly_rate
INPUT hours_worked

IF hours_worked <= 40
    regular_pay = hours_worked * hourly_rate
    overtime_pay = 0
ELSE
    regular_pay = 40 * hourly_rate
    overtime_hours = hours_worked - 40
    overtime_pay = overtime_hours * hourly_rate * 1.5
END IF

gross_pay = regular_pay + overtime_pay
DISPLAY gross_pay
This exposes the algorithmic logic, conditions, calculations, variables, and execution order.

Level 4: Source code
Translates the algorithm into an actual programming language, such as C++.

Important terminology
"High-level abstraction" is a descriptive expression, not necessarily the name of a standardized pseudocode technique.

Relevant established concepts include:

Computational abstraction.
Problem-level representation.
Design-level representation.
Top-down problem solving.
Algorithmic decomposition.
Black-boxing.
Proposed terminology for this study: High-level problem representation or design-level representation.

The purpose is to provide students with an understanding of the major responsibilities of a solution without immediately revealing its detailed algorithm.

5. Which Decisions Should AI Expose, and Which Should Students Develop?
A possible instructional division is to organize programming decisions into three groups.

Group A: Decisions AI can make explicit
These decisions help students understand and organize the problem without directly revealing its solution.

Decision AI's role
Problem understanding Clarify what the problem asks.
Input identification Help identify the required information.
Output identification Clarify expected results.
Constraint identification Highlight important requirements and restrictions.
Problem decomposition Identify major responsibilities and subgoals.
For example, AI can tell a student:

"Your payroll program needs to collect employee information, determine earnings, account for overtime, and display gross pay."

This provides direction without revealing the overtime formula or the condition that determines when overtime applies.

Group B: Decisions students should actively develop
These represent the central reasoning activities that the study aims to preserve.

Decision Student's responsibility
Data modeling Decide how information should be represented.
Algorithmic approach Develop an approach to solving the problem.
Computational logic Determine the necessary calculations and operations.
Conditional reasoning Identify situations requiring different outcomes.
Control-flow selection Determine how the program should organize those situations.
Execution order Organize the solution into a logical sequence.
These decisions should not necessarily be completed without assistance.

Instead, the AI should guide students toward developing and explaining their own decisions.

Group C: Decisions that can be shared
Decision Student's responsibility AI's responsibility
Data type selection Propose appropriate data types. Explain choices and trade-offs.
Control structure selection Suggest an approach. Explain alternatives.
Edge-case identification Propose scenarios. Suggest missing cases.
Debugging Diagnose the problem. Provide diagnostic hints.
Testing Select test cases. Help evaluate test coverage.
The objective is to develop students' ability to make and justify programming decisions, rather than requiring them to work without any assistance.

6. The Problem with Withholding the Algorithmic Approach
One important concern is that beginners may not know how to develop an algorithmic approach independently.

Suppose an AI tutor always asks:

"How would you calculate overtime?"

A beginner might not know where to start.

If the tutor instead provides:

"Use an if-else statement and calculate overtime using (hours - 40) × rate × 1.5."

The tutor has already exposed much of the algorithm.

Neither extreme is necessarily ideal.

The instructional challenge is to provide enough support to help students progress while preserving their involvement in developing the solution.

This leads to the idea of progressive scaffolding.

7. Progressive Scaffolding in AI Tutoring
Rather than always revealing or always withholding the algorithm, the tutor can progressively increase the level of assistance depending on the student's understanding.

Example tutoring interaction
Problem: Calculate employee gross pay, including overtime.

Tutor — Initial support:

The problem mentions regular hours and overtime. Do you think every hour worked should be compensated at the same rate?

Student:

No, overtime hours have a different rate.

Tutor — Guiding question:

What information from the problem determines when regular working hours end and overtime begins?

Student:

The 40-hour threshold.

Tutor — Deeper reasoning:

Suppose an employee works 45 hours. Should the overtime rate apply to all 45 hours or only some of them? Why?

Student:

Only the five hours beyond 40.

Tutor — Transition:

Now organize the regular and overtime calculations into a sequence that produces the final gross pay.

In this interaction:

The tutor clarifies the requirements.
The student identifies the relevant distinction.
The tutor guides the student toward the threshold.
The student reasons through the calculation.
The student develops the solution structure.
The tutor supports the reasoning process without immediately presenting the complete algorithm.

Progressive assistance levels
A possible tutoring strategy:

Clarification: Explain the problem requirements.
Guidance: Ask questions that direct attention toward relevant information.
Hints: Provide increasingly specific clues.
Partial explanation: Explain a difficult concept or decision.
Worked guidance: Demonstrate part of the reasoning when necessary.
Implementation support: Help translate the student's developed approach into code.
The goal is not to make students struggle unnecessarily. It is to help them move toward independent problem-solving.

9. Proposed Research Framework
The following is a proposed framework for investigating AI-supported programming problem-solving.

AI-supported programming problem-solving
Stage 1: Well-defined programming problem

The student receives a problem with clear requirements, constraints, and expected outcomes.

Stage 2: High-level problem representation

AI exposes the major responsibilities and subgoals without immediately revealing detailed implementation logic.

Stage 3: Student programming decisions

The student actively develops:

Input and output modeling.
Data modeling.
Problem decomposition.
Algorithmic approach.
Conditional and computational logic.
Control flow and execution order.
Testing strategy.
AI provides guidance, hints, and explanations when needed.

Stage 4: Implementation

The student translates the developed solution into C++.

Stage 5: Testing and evaluation

The student verifies the implementation and evaluates whether the solution satisfies the requirements.

Stage 6: Independent assessment

The student's ability to solve a new programming problem without AI assistance is evaluated.

10. Proposed Research Questions
The central research question can be expressed as:

How can AI tutoring techniques support CS1 students in developing programming solutions while preserving their active involvement in problem-solving decisions?

Possible supporting research questions:

RQ1: How do different AI tutoring approaches influence CS1 students' engagement in programming problem-solving activities?

RQ2: How do different AI tutoring approaches influence students' ability to independently develop programming solutions?

RQ3: How do students interact with AI guidance when making programming decisions?

The final wording should depend on the actual experimental conditions, assessment methods, and research objectives.

11. Potential Learning Outcomes and Evaluation
The study should not measure success only by whether students complete programming assignments with AI assistance.

A student might successfully complete an assignment because the AI effectively provided the solution.

Another student might take longer, receive hints, make mistakes, and eventually develop the solution independently.

The second process may demonstrate more meaningful learning.

Possible evaluation dimensions:

Evaluation dimension What it measures
Programming decision quality Whether students make appropriate decisions.
Decision justification Whether students can explain why they made those decisions.
Independent problem-solving Whether students can solve problems without AI.
Transfer ability Whether students can apply their reasoning to a new problem.
Engagement How actively students participate in the tutoring process.
AI dependency How much students rely on AI to make decisions for them.
An independent or transfer assessment is especially important if the study aims to make claims about retained problem-solving ability.

12. Important Research Design Distinction
Two separate dimensions should be maintained.

Programming decisions
These describe what the student needs to reason about.

Examples:

Problem decomposition.
Algorithmic approach.
Data modeling.
Conditional reasoning.
Testing strategy.
Engagement technique
This describes how the AI tutor interacts with the student.

Examples:

Lead-and-Reveal.
Explain-Before-Usage.
Guided questioning.
Progressive hints.
Conversational explanations.
This distinction is important when comparing different AI tutoring approaches.

The same programming decisions can be examined under different engagement techniques.

13. Central Research Contribution
The research should not simply investigate whether AI should reveal or hide pseudocode, algorithms, or source code.

A more meaningful direction is to investigate:

How AI can scaffold students through programming decisions without taking ownership of those decisions away from them.

The objective is to find an appropriate balance between AI assistance and student reasoning.

The tutor should not leave beginners without enough guidance to make progress. At the same time, it should not make every important problem-solving decision on their behalf.

The desired outcome is not merely successful AI-assisted code generation. It is the development of students who can independently understand problems, make programming decisions, develop algorithms, implement solutions, and transfer their problem-solving abilities to new problems.
