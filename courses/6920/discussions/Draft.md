
Case Study #1Trading at the Speed of Light: Nasdaq’s Information Challenges
                
                
            The world’s largest “floorless” exchange handles hundreds of millions of trades every day. Buys and sells happen so quickly that each trade has to be time-stamped to the nanosecond. First launched in 2000, Nasdaq is, above all, a technology company, and it successfully competes against the venerable New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) on its breathtaking trading speed.When most people think of an exchange, they think of NYSE’s enormous building on Wall Street, with loud-mouthed traders on the floor shouting orders, racing stock tickers, and giant LCD screens laden with charts, numbers, and ticker symbols. In fact, most exchanges are in data centers, not in neoclassical buildings. And they are also for-profit businesses that compete for companies to list their shares and for investors and brokers to conduct their trades.Speed matters, and Nasdaq technology can handle one million messages per second. It matters so much that some heavy traders—Goldman Sachs, for example—paid Nasdaq for the privilege of locating their own server in Nasdaq’s data center, just to avoid the tiny communication delay from Goldman offices. A trader’s servers can instantly detect any delay and can then automatically check other exchanges to see if the trade can be rerouted. As Nasdaq moves its servers to the cloud, traders like Goldman Sachs can move their servers to the cloud provider’s data center.With traders submitting orders from all over the world, however, Nasdaq must work toward even faster response times. The company must also find ways to accurately judge the order in which those buy and sell commands are submitted so that proximity to Nasdaq’s data center would no longer provide an unfair advantage. Computer scientists at Stanford and Google are working on a newer technology that can track time down to 100 billionths of a second, which is considerably faster than anything in place yet. This new technology allows each computer to add a timestamp to each order as it is placed, so even if orders arrive out of sequence due to distance, they can be reorganized into the correct sequence.High-Frequency TradingAnother looming issue for Nasdaq is computer trading based on algorithms, or “algo-trading,” a trend that may only grow with ever-faster trading. With machines talking to machines, racing with one another to close the deal at the best price, trading volume can skyrocket quickly. Humans use their judgment to craft the mathematical rules; once in place, however, the systems can trigger frenzied rounds of trading.Some argue that these high-frequency trades make the markets more efficient and equitable so that the big players on the trading floor don’t have an advantage. While Nasdaq and other exchanges compete for the growing number of algo-traders, analysts worry that the sheer technological speed introduces serious risks. When markets dropped a gut-wrenching 9% on one afternoon in May, 2010, for example, some suspected a clumsy algo-trader who accidentally triggered the event (Figure 1.23). Although the real cause of that roller-coaster “flash crash” was never clear, such programmed trades might well be at fault.

            But algo-traders defend their strategy by pointing out that they could not easily trade large numbers of shares at fair prices without the “speed of light” because the trades would signal their intent to other traders: Once that happened, the prices would change dramatically.Since the “flash crash,” Nasdaq and the other exchanges have strengthened the guardrails to better safeguard against such events affecting stocks. For instance, in addition to the “circuit breakers” that stop trading when prices fall below a certain level, exchanges prevent high-frequency traders from moving stock shares too quickly if prices go outside previously set “bands.” For stock investors, these measures provide some reassurance. But investors who buy and sell cryptocurrencies don’t have such a safety net yet because those assets and the technology underlying them are not under the control of a company like Nasdaq. Breathtaking price swings of cryptocurrencies are not uncommon.The exchanges and the government agencies that oversee them continue to explore new strategies, and they also take tough stands against suspected misconduct. But unlike the Nasdaq trades, their work is not at the speed of light. Discussion Questions

Case Study #2Breaking News: Twitter’s Growing Role in Emergencies and Disaster CommunicationsWhen a city council member in Atlanta, Georgia, spotted a woman on the street suffering a seizure, he quickly pulled out his cell phone. The display indicated that it was low on battery, so instead of dialing 911 and getting stuck on hold, Council Member Hall tapped out a short tweet:“Need a paramedic on corner of John Wesley Dobbs and Jackson st. Woman on the ground unconscious. Pls ReTweet”Several of the council member’s followers immediately saw his text message and dialed 911. Paramedics arrived quickly to take the woman to the hospital.Twitter, the microblogging service best known for trivial updates on everyday events and brutal political jibes, has a growing role in emergency response. The service empowers people with the ability to gather and disseminate information about emergencies and disasters, and this information can be far timelier than anything government authorities or organizations such as the Red Cross can provide.During hurricanes and typhoons, for example, millions of tweets can track the location and timing of flooding events, rising in volume with the water. In an earthquake situation, people can report whether they feel the jolt or shaking and how strong it is. The short messages offer on-the-ground observations of conditions, along with the worry and fear for missing family members. They also pass along—or “retweet”—official news from regular sources, such as TV or state government.
                
                
            One important advantage of Twitter messages during natural disasters is that they can provide first-hand accounts and real-time updates about conditions in each neighborhood, often calling for immediate rescue for individuals in peril. In the aftermath of a major earthquake in Haiti, for example, a graduate student at the University of Colorado launched a project to improve Twitter’s usefulness, called Tweak the Tweet. Kate Starbird aimed to develop syntax for tweets originating from the disaster sites so they could be better organized and read by computer programs (Figure 1.24). The freewheeling, unstructured tweets about victims who needed help were repurposed into more structured messages with “hash tags”—keywords preceded by a pound sign (#). Computer programs can read these to categorize who is involved, what is needed, where the problem is, and what else might be happening.

        Restructured tweets, however, require Twitter users to know more than most users do and to use that knowledge when they are under considerable stress. Researchers are also working on strategies to deploy machine learning and artificial intelligence to sift through millions of tweets in a way that will uncover detailed information about people in need or collapsing buildings during disasters. For instance, one approach teases out details about smaller events, such as specific needs for food, medicine, or rescue. The algorithms catch phrases such as “person trapped” or “bridge collapse” to home in on events that may need immediate response.The Red Cross and other disaster response organizations recognize that people are relying more heavily on social media such as Twitter for support and information during emergencies. In fact, social media are so prevalent that, in one survey, more than a quarter of the respondents said they would send a direct Twitter message to emergency responders, not realizing that aid organizations are still not well prepared to monitor Twitter and other social media services. They also have few means to assess the value of information received in this way. It may be timely, but is it accurate? It might be a child’s exaggerated report or a hoax from some scammer. As artificial intelligence strategies improve to monitor and analyze tweets, aid organizations will be able to take better advantage of the input from social media.Despite the drawbacks, Twitter’s value for emergency response and disaster communications is promising. In a study of Twitter use during a variety of emergencies, researchers found that about 42% of the tweets were posted by traditional and internet news outlets. Critical eyewitness accounts made up 9%. Craig Fugate, an administrator at the Federal Emergency Management Center, said, “Social media can empower the public to be part of the response, not as victims to be taken care of.” Discussion Questions
